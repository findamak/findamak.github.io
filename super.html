Fast summary

The big distinction is phase: ordinary income and capital gains in accumulation are concessionally taxed, while earnings on assets supporting a qualifying retirement-phase pension are generally exempt from ordinary fund tax. The personal transfer balance cap limits transfers into retirement phase, not subsequent investment growth. The FY2026-27 general cap is $2.1m, but an existing pension holder’s personal cap may be lower. Division 296 is a separate member-level tax.

Accumulation long-term CGT 10%

Pension ordinary income 0%

Division 296 adds extra tax for high total super balances from 2026-27: 15% on earnings attributable to TSB above $3m, plus an additional 10% on earnings attributable to TSB above $10m.

Tax on super

There are two separate layers: ordinary fund tax depends on the account phase; additional Division 296 tax depends on total super balance. These taxes apply to income and realised capital gains, subject to the relevant exemptions and adjustments—not to the super balance itself. For Division 296, total super balance determines what proportion of the statutory earnings base is subject to additional tax.

1. Ordinary fund tax: depends on the account phase

These rates apply to earnings, not your super balance. Accumulation tax applies regardless of whether your total super is below $2.1m, between $2.1m and $3m, or above $3m.

Account phaseOrdinary taxable incomeEligible long-term capital gains
Accumulation earnings — at any total super balance Generally 15% fund tax Generally 10% effective fund tax after the one-third CGT discount for assets held at least 12 months
Qualifying retirement-phase pension earnings Generally 0% ordinary fund tax Generally 0% ordinary fund tax

Ordinary income includes interest, dividends, distributions and rent. Shorter-held capital gains do not receive the one-third discount. Deductions, losses and tax offsets can affect the result; special rules such as non-arm’s-length income are outside this ordinary-tax summary.

The $2.1m general transfer balance cap is not a tax-free balance band. It limits transfers into retirement phase in FY2026-27. Your personal cap may be lower, and subsequent investment growth can take a pension balance above the cap without automatically moving the excess into accumulation.

2. Additional Division 296 tax: depends on total super balance

This is a separate member-level tax, additional to ordinary fund tax. It uses total super across accumulation and pension interests and applies to a calculated proportion of statutory Division 296 earnings. Ordinary accumulation tax continues to apply above the Division 296 thresholds.

Reference TSB — FY2026-27Additional Division 296 treatment
$3m or less No Division 296 tax. Ordinary fund tax can still apply to accumulation earnings.
Above $3m (including balances above $10m) 15% additional tax on the proportion of Division 296 earnings attributable to the balance above $3m.
Above $10m The above-$3m charge still applies, plus another 10% on the proportion of Division 296 earnings attributable to the balance above $10m.

For FY2026-27, reference TSB means the Division 296 total super balance at 30 June 2027, excluding LRBA amounts. These thresholds are subject to future indexation. The rates apply to the adjusted statutory earnings base, not directly to gross capital gains or sale proceeds. Specific exemptions can apply. See the worked examples and balance-date rules for the calculation and treatment in later years.

Accumulation tax does not start at $2.1m or stop at $3m. Pension tax treatment depends on the phase; Division 296 is a separate additional tax for high total balances.

Modelling: Division 296 worked examples

These examples isolate the additional member-level Division 296 tax. They assume $100,000 of total super earnings for Division 296 purposes in each case, already calculated and attributed across the member’s funds and interests after statutory adjustments. This is an illustrative input, not an assumed investment return, sale price or capital gain, and it does not exclude pension earnings.

Balance thresholds and earnings are separate inputs

B is the Division 296 reference total super balance; E is the member’s total super earnings for Division 296 purposes, before the threshold proportions are applied. For these positive-earnings examples:

  • Above-$3m percentage = max(0, (B − $3m) / B × 100), rounded to two decimal places.
  • Above-$10m percentage = max(0, (B − $10m) / B × 100), rounded to two decimal places.
  • Additional tax = E × above-$3m percentage × 15% + E × above-$10m percentage × 10% (use percentages as fractions in multiplication).

For FY2026-27, B is the TSB at 30 June 2027. From FY2027-28, B is generally the greater of opening and closing TSB. Division 296 excludes LRBA amounts from this balance calculation. The $3m and $10m thresholds are indexed in $150,000 and $500,000 increments respectively in line with CPI; use the applicable year’s thresholds. Specific exclusions and death-year rules can alter liability.

Why pension earnings are not simply excluded

Division 296 fund earnings are an adjusted tax measure, not merely the fund’s taxable accumulation income. Section 296-60 adds net exempt current pension income and adjusts the treatment of pension-asset capital gains. Funds calculate and attribute relevant earnings to members; the ATO combines those amounts before applying the balance proportions. The 15% and 10% additional rates in the table above apply to that statutory earnings base, not directly to undiscounted sale proceeds or gross capital gains.

Transitional CGT adjustments: fund type matters

  • SMSFs and small APRA funds: an election can use 30 June 2026 market value for the first element of eligible assets’ cost bases/reduced cost bases for Division 296 purposes, with other pre-existing elements reset to nil. The election applies to all eligible CGT assets held at that date, must be made by the due date of the fund’s FY2026-27 annual return, and cannot be revoked. Direct ownership and limited look-through rules apply.
  • Large APRA funds: net capital gains are multiplied by 0.2, 0.4, 0.6 and 0.8 for FY2026-27, FY2027-28, FY2028-29 and FY2029-30 respectively when working out Division 296 earnings. No further phase-in adjustment applies after FY2029-30.
  • These adjustments are for Division 296 only, not a reset of ordinary fund CGT. The $100,000 earnings input above is assumed to be the final attributed amount after any applicable adjustment; do not apply the CGT discount or phase-in multiplier to it again.

See the current ATO calculation guide, SMSF/APRA guidance and legislation linked below. An actual sale model needs fund-specific inputs and, where required, actuarial attribution; the examples deliberately do not substitute a guessed earnings base.

Selling assets in pension phase

A worked FY2026-27 example showing why $0 ordinary fund CGT does not necessarily mean $0 total tax. Unlike the threshold examples above, this example derives the Division 296 earnings from an asset sale. All figures and circumstances below are hypothetical, not a calculation of your personal liability.

Explicit assumptions

  • A complying single-member SMSF supports only the member’s qualifying retirement-phase account-based pension throughout the year. The pension was lawfully commenced and grew through investment returns, not excess transfers. Minimum pension payments and all exemption requirements are met, including any required actuarial certification; the sale gain is fully covered by the ordinary pension earnings exemption.
  • The member has no other super interests. Their Division 296 reference TSB at 30 June 2027 is exactly $10,000,000, after the year’s transactions. This is a separate supplied balance input, not the sale proceeds or an amount reconstructed from this one asset.
  • The SMSF directly owns an eligible CGT asset bought in May 2020 with an ordinary tax cost base of $600,000. Its market value on 30 June 2026 is $900,000. It is sold in May 2027 for $1,200,000, having been held for at least 12 months and qualifying for the fund’s one-third CGT discount.
  • The SMSF makes a valid transitional CGT election by the required deadline. The Division 296 cost base for this asset is therefore assumed to be $900,000, with no further cost-base adjustments. The election applies to all eligible assets, not just this one. This uses the SMSF election, not the large-APRA-fund phase-in.
  • There are no other earnings, current or carried-forward capital/tax losses, expenses, contributions, non-arm’s-length income or other statutory adjustments affecting the example. All resulting Division 296 earnings are attributed to this member, with any required actuarial attribution completed. No personal exclusion from Division 296 applies.

Contribution caps

For FY2026-27, concessional contributions are capped at $32,500 and are generally taxed at 15% in the fund. Non-concessional contributions are capped at $130,000 annually, with bring-forward access based on total super balance immediately before the financial year (30 June 2026 for FY2026-27). These bring-forward bands assume you are eligible to start a new arrangement; an existing bring-forward arrangement does not automatically reset to the new caps.

Contributing is not the same as claiming a deduction. At ages 67–74, a deduction for personal super contributions requires the work test or exemption. Non-concessional personal contributions and salary-sacrifice contributions do not have that same work-test requirement. To claim a personal deduction, provide a valid notice of intent and receive the fund’s acknowledgement within the applicable deadlines, before commencing a pension or making a rollover/withdrawal that invalidates the notice. Age and receipt-window restrictions still apply.

Withdrawal tax from a taxed super fund

Withdrawal tax depends on age, tax-free versus taxable components, and whether the benefit is paid as a lump sum or pension income stream. Preservation age is birth-date based: 55 for people born before 1 July 1960, then rising by one year per cohort to 60 for anyone born from 1 July 1964.

Pension phase: starting, contributing and withdrawing

An account-based pension is a separate super interest. Once started, it cannot receive further contributions or rollovers; it must also meet the annual minimum pension-payment rules.

When can an account-based pension start?

You generally need a full condition of release: reach age 65; permanently retire after reaching preservation age; or cease an employment arrangement after turning 60. Permanent incapacity and a terminal medical condition can also give access under separate rules.

A transition-to-retirement income stream can start from preservation age, but has different rules: generally no lump-sum commutations and a 10% annual maximum payment until a full condition of release is met.

Annual minimum withdrawal

The minimum is normally calculated from the pension balance at 1 July (or is pro-rated in its first year):

  • Under 65: 4%
  • 65–74: 5%
  • 75–79: 6%
  • 80–84: 7%
  • 85–89: 9%
  • 90–94: 11%
  • 95 or over: 14%
Contributions after pension commencement

Employer, salary-sacrifice, personal and rollover money cannot be added to an existing pension account. If your fund accepts a later contribution, it is normally held in a new or existing accumulation account under the same member login.

You can later start a separate account-based pension from that accumulation balance if you meet a condition of release and have enough remaining personal transfer balance cap space.

More than one pension account

Having super in a retail or industry fund and in an SMSF will commonly mean two pension accounts if you start a pension from each fund. They remain separate accounts administered by separate trustees, but they share one personal transfer balance cap; two accounts do not give you two caps. A later accumulation account can also become a second pension account rather than being added to the first.

The exact retirement, employment-cessation, incapacity, TTR, death-benefit and transfer-balance-cap rules can differ by circumstance and fund. Confirm the intended pension commencement and rollover steps with the fund and an appropriately licensed adviser before acting.

Super death-benefit tax

Australia has no general inheritance tax, but a super death benefit can be taxed when it is paid to a person who is not a tax dependant of the deceased. This makes the tax-component mix of each person's super an important estate-planning issue.

Tax-free component 0%

This usually includes non-concessional (after-tax) contributions, government co-contributions and spouse contributions. It remains tax-free when paid as a lump-sum death benefit, including to an independent adult child.

Taxable component — taxed element Up to 17%

This commonly includes employer and salary-sacrifice contributions, deductible personal contributions and fund earnings. A lump sum to an independent adult child is taxed at up to 15% plus Medicare levy where applicable.

Taxable component — untaxed element Up to 32%

This can arise in particular from insurance proceeds or untaxed funds. A lump sum to an independent adult child can be taxed at up to 30% plus Medicare levy where applicable.

Recipient when you die General lump-sum treatment from a taxed fund Planning point
Tax dependant Generally tax-free, including both tax-free and taxable components. A spouse or de facto spouse, a former spouse or former de facto spouse, child under 18, financially dependent person, or a person in an interdependency relationship can be a tax dependant. An adult child can also qualify if financially dependent or interdependent at death.
Independent adult child Tax-free component: 0%. Taxed element: up to 15% + Medicare levy. Untaxed element: up to 30% + Medicare levy. Medicare levy applies where applicable. Legal personal representative / estate The estate's treatment depends on who is expected to benefit. Paying through an estate does not automatically make death-benefit tax disappear. It can help implement a will or protect a beneficiary's circumstances, but can add delay, cost and potential estate-dispute exposure. Obtain legal and tax advice before choosing this route.
1. Review each member separately

Super is not one family pool: each member has their own tax-free and taxable components, beneficiaries, nominations and access conditions. Request the component breakdown for each account, including any SMSF interest, and review it alongside binding nominations and the will.

2. Re-contribution strategy

Where a member can lawfully withdraw super and has non-concessional contribution capacity, they may withdraw an amount and contribute it back as an after-tax contribution. The re-contributed amount becomes tax-free component, which can reduce tax if an independent adult child later receives the benefit.

It is constrained by release rules, age, total-super-balance tests, annual/bring-forward caps, timing and fund processes. It can also leave money outside super while the steps are completed.

3. Withdraw before death

Once lawfully withdrawn, money is outside the super death-benefit system and may form part of the estate, depending on ownership and subsequent arrangements; it does not automatically pass under the will. This may avoid super death-benefit tax for an independent adult child, but only if the member can access the money while alive.

Cashing out early can forfeit super's concessional tax treatment and may affect Centrelink, asset protection, investment structure and estate outcomes. It is not a simple last-minute strategy.

Important distinctions: super-law dependants (who a fund may pay) and tax dependants (who receive concessional death-benefit tax treatment) are related but not identical concepts. Death-benefit income streams, defined-benefit and untaxed funds, insurance proceeds, foreign beneficiaries and complex estates can have different rules. Do not make withdrawals, re-contributions or nominations solely from this page; obtain licensed financial and estate-planning advice.
